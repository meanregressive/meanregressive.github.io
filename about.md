I grew up in Kolkata, where I studied English literature at Jadavpur University. The discipline trained me to read carefully — to attend to what a text says, what it omits, what it assumes about its audience. I carried that habit into a world that runs increasingly on datasets rather than documents, and I have found it surprisingly useful there too.

After graduating, I worked for two years at Quest Alliance in Mumbai, building monitoring and evaluation systems for vocational training programs across India. It was my first encounter with the gap between a program's theory of change and its measured outcomes — a gap I have spent the better part of a decade trying to understand. In 2017 I moved to Washington to study International Economics and International Relations at the School of Advanced International Studies at Johns Hopkins University. SAIS gave me the policy frame: how do institutions shape the choices available to poor households? How do global economic forces translate — or fail to translate — into better lives at the village level?

From 2019 to 2023 I worked at Equimundo, an organization focused on gender equality and the prevention of violence. There I ran research across more than thirty countries, harmonizing survey data, leading landscape reports, and collaborating with country teams on how to make the findings useful for programs and policy. The work sharpened my sense of what rigorous qualitative and mixed-method research can and cannot establish.

Since 2023 I have been a Research Analyst at IFPRI, where I do primarily quantitative work: impact evaluations of randomized controlled trials, administrative data analysis at scale, and systematic reviews. I work across Bangladesh, Somalia, Mozambique, and Egypt. The questions remain the same as they were at Quest Alliance — what actually changes people's lives? — but the methods have become more formal.

In 2025 I completed graduate coursework in Linear Algebra, Multivariable Calculus, and Real Analysis at American University. I intend to pursue a PhD in Economics. These courses were not a detour; they were preparation. The arc from literature to international relations to microdata to real analysis is not accidental — it reflects a sustained attempt to build the right tools for the questions I care about.
